A quantum system can exist in a superposition where multiple possibilities coexist coherently.
After decoherence, these possibilities no longer interfere. In each resulting branch, observers
experience a single definite, classical outcome.

Quantum superposition → Coherence → Decoherence → Classical-looking outcomes (or branches in Many-Worlds).

Copenhagen interpretation: Decoherence is associated with one outcome becoming the observed reality 
                            (often described alongside wavefunction collapse).
Many-Worlds interpretation: There is no collapse. Decoherence simply marks the point where different 
                            components of the quantum state stop interfering and behave like independent branches.

The Many-Worlds Interpretation is one way of interpreting quantum mechanics.
    It says:
        The wavefunction never collapses. Instead, every possible quantum outcome continues to exist in 
        different branches of the universe.
        This is an interpretation, not an experimentally proven fact. Other interpretations 
        (such as the Copenhagen interpretation) explain the same experiments differently.


Does every decision create a new universe? Not exactly.

    A common popular explanation is:
        "Every decision creates a new universe."
    That's an oversimplification.

    A more accurate statement is:
        Quantum events create branches. If human decisions ultimately depend on those 
        quantum events, then different branches can contain different decisions.

    So it isn't your consciousness that creates branches. It's the quantum evolution of the physical universe.


What is a quantum event?

    A quantum event is an event at the microscopic level where quantum mechanics allows multiple possible outcomes.

    Examples:
        A radioactive atom may decay or not.
        An electron's spin may be measured as Up or Down.
        A photon may pass through one slit or another.

        These are genuine quantum events.


Quantum system
    A quantum system allows multiple possibilities to coexist.

    Example:
        Electron
            Spin Up + Spin Down
        Or
        Photon
            Left Path + Right Path

        These possibilities still belong to one quantum state.


Coherence means:
    Different quantum possibilities can still interfere with each other.
    The double-slit experiment is the classic example.

    Electron:
            Electron
                |
        -------------
        |           |
    Left Slit   Right Slit
        \           /
        \         /
            \       /
            Screen

    The two paths interfere.
    This interference proves they are still coherent.


Decoherence
    Decoherence happens when the quantum system interacts with its environment.

    For example:
        Detector
        Air molecules
        Heat
        Light
        Dust

    Information spreads into the environment.
    Now the possibilities stop interfering.

        Instead of
        Alive + Dead

        we now have
        Universe
            |
        --------------
        |            |
        Alive      Dead

        The branches become effectively independent.


Why does decoherence matter?
    Because after decoherence:

    The branches no longer interfere.
    They behave like separate classical realities.
    We observe one definite outcome in each branch.

    Decoherence explains why we don't see:
    Half-dead cats
    Half-raining skies
    Half-moving cars

    Everyday objects appear classical because decoherence happens incredibly quickly.


Does decoherence create branching?
    Almost.

    A more precise statement is:
        The quantum state is already evolving.

    Decoherence explains when the different parts stop interfering.
    Many-Worlds interprets those non-interacting parts as separate branches.


Classical system
    A classical system appears to have only one definite outcome.

    Examples:
        The cat is alive.
        It is raining.
        The light is ON.
    There is no observable superposition.


The Big Picture
    The flow of ideas is:

        Quantum System
            ↓
        Superposition
        (Multiple possibilities)
            ↓
        Coherence
        (Possibilities can interfere)
            ↓
        Interaction with Environment
            ↓
        Decoherence
        (Interference disappears)
            ↓
        Many-Worlds Interpretation:
        Branches become effectively independent
                OR
        Copenhagen Interpretation:
        Wavefunction collapses to one outcome



    
    Key Takeaways:-
        Many-Worlds is an interpretation, not a proven fact.
        Quantum events, not consciousness, are what underlie branching.
        A superposition means multiple possibilities coexist in one quantum state.
        Coherence means those possibilities can still interfere with each other.
        Decoherence happens when interactions with the environment prevent that interference, 
            making the possibilities behave like separate classical realities.
        In Many-Worlds, decoherence corresponds to effectively separate branches rather than a collapse.
        Your beliefs or intentions do not determine which branch you experience.
        If two branches exist, both versions of you are equally real in MWI, but each 
            can experience only their own branch.
        Every branch feels like a normal classical world to the people living inside it.


Can we recombine Classical Outcomes ??
    The short answer is:
        Quantum theory says recombination is possible only if coherence is preserved. 
        Once decoherence has occurred, recombination is theoretically possible but 
        practically impossible for macroscopic objects.

    Let's unpack that.

    Recombining in a quantum system
        Consider the double-slit experiment.

        An electron travels toward two slits.
        Before it reaches the screen:

            Electron

                |
            ----------
            |        |
            Left     Right

        The electron is in a coherent superposition of both paths.
        Because coherence is maintained, the two paths recombine at the screen.

        When they recombine, they interfere:
            Some locations become more likely.
            Others become less likely.

        This interference is direct evidence that the two possibilities were never truly independent.


    What if you detect which slit it went through?

        Now you place a detector at the slits.

            Electron
                |
            Detector observes path

        The electron becomes entangled with the detector and the environment.
        Coherence is lost (decoherence).

        Now there is no interference pattern.
        The two paths no longer recombine.

        Why not?

        Because the information about the path has spread into the environment.
        It's no longer just:
        Electron

        It's now something like:
            Electron
            +
            Detector
            +
            Air molecules
            +
            Photons
            +
            Heat
            +
            Everything nearby

        To recombine the two branches, you'd have to reverse every one of those interactions.
        TO RECOMBINE the TWO BRANCHES, you'd have to REVERSE EVERY ONE OF THOSE INTERACTIONS.


    Cat example
        Initially:
            Atom
            Decay + No Decay

        After interacting with the cat:
            Dead Cat + Alive Cat

        After one second:
            Air molecules bounce off the cat.
            Heat leaves the cat.
            Light reflects from the cat.
            Dust particles interact.

        Now the state involves an astronomical number of particles.

        To recombine the "dead cat" and "alive cat" branches, you'd have to put every particle 
        in the universe that recorded information about the cat back into exactly the right quantum state.

        The laws of quantum mechanics don't forbid this in principle, but for anything 
        macroscopic it's so unimaginably difficult that it's effectively impossible.
    
    
    Does Many-Worlds say branches can merge?

        In principle:
            Yes, because the universal wavefunction always evolves according to the same 
            reversible quantum equations (the Schrödinger equation).

        If two branches could somehow be brought back into perfect coherence, they could interfere again.

        In practice:
            No one knows how to do this for macroscopic objects, and it is considered 
            effectively impossible because of decoherence.



A note on "classical outcomes"

    When we say "classical outcomes" (like "the cat is alive" or "the cat is dead"), we're talking about
    states after decoherence. At that point, they're no longer behaving as coherent quantum alternatives.

    So it's more accurate to say:
        Quantum states can recombine if coherence is maintained.
        Classical-looking outcomes arise because decoherence has made recombination effectively impossible.

    That's why the everyday world appears to have definite histories, even though the underlying quantum theory is reversible.
