Paralysis is the loss of voluntary muscle movement, which can be temporary or permanent, and is 
classified by the body parts affected and the underlying neurological cause.
    Types of Paralysis
        1. Monoplegia: Affects a single limb, such as one arm or leg, often resulting from localized 
            nerve injury or stroke 
        2. Hemiplegia: Involves one side of the body, typically an arm and leg on the same side, commonly 
            caused by strokes or brain injuries 
        3. Paraplegia: Affects both legs and sometimes the lower torso, usually due to spinal cord 
            injuries in the thoracic, lumbar, or sacral regions 
        4. Quadriplegia (Tetraplegia): Involves all four limbs and the torso, often resulting 
            from cervical spinal cord injuries, which may also impair breathing and arm movement

The most common causes are strokes and spinal cord injuries. Other primary disorders and conditions include:
    Neurological & Motor Neuron Diseases: Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS), Cerebral Palsy, and 
        Spinal Muscular Atrophy.
    Autoimmune & Demyelinating Disorders: Multiple Sclerosis (MS) and [Guillain-Barré Syndrome.]
    Infections: Polio, meningitis, and sometimes complications from Lyme disease or tick bites.
    Genetic & Congenital Conditions: Muscular dystrophy and spina bifida.
    Other Causes: Brain/spinal tumors, severe peripheral neuropathy, or brief post-seizure episodes (Todd’s paralysis)

Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) is a rare neurological disorder where the body’s immune system 
    mistakenly attacks its own peripheral nerves. This attack—usually triggered by a recent viral
    or bacterial infection—causes sudden muscle weakness, tingling, and potentially paralysis. 
    While severe cases can be life-threatening, most people recover fully with medical treatment.

Functional Neurological Disorder (FND) is a condition where the brain struggles to send and 
    receive signals properly. Think of it like computer software experiencing a glitch while 
    the hardware (the brain) remains perfectly intact. Symptoms are genuine, involuntary, and 
    include limb weakness, tremors, and nonepileptic.
    functional neurological disorder (FND) can cause paralysis or severe weakness in the 
    arms, legs, or one entire side of the body.

Todd’s paralysis (or Todd’s paresis) is temporary weakness or paralysis that affects a person 
    immediately following an epileptic seizure. It usually impacts just one side of the body or a 
    specific limb. Symptoms, which can also include vision issues or slurred speech, 
    resolve entirely within 48 hours.
    Location: It generally affects the same side of the body that was impacted by the seizure.
    Duration: Symptoms typically last between 30 minutes and 36 hours, averaging about 15 hours.
    Range: It can present as slight muscle weakness or a complete inability to move or feel the affected limb.
    Sensory issues: Some individuals may experience speech difficulties (aphasia), blurred vision, or visual loss.

Difference between seizure and Fits:- 
    "Fit" is a common, non-medical term typically used to describe a seizure that involves violent, 
    uncontrollable shaking and loss of consciousness. A "seizure" is the broader, clinical term 
    for any sudden, temporary burst of abnormal electrical activity in the brain, ranging from 
    subtle staring spells to full-body convulsions


What Actually Happens When You Overload Your Brain:
    When you push your brain beyond its working memory limit, 
    it begins to shut down rather than suffer physical structural damage.
    You may experience:
    Cognitive Fatigue & Brain Fog: A sharp decline in performance, 
        slower recall, and an inability to think clearly.
    Stress Response: Your body may release stress hormones like cortisol, leading to anxiety, 
        irritability, and sleep disturbances.
    Paralysis: The brain struggles to filter data, resulting in indecision and an inability 
        to focus on new tasks.
    Physical Symptoms: Mental exhaustion can manifest as physical tension, eye strain, 
        headaches, and physical fatigue.

    Immense or chronic stress does not directly cause brain hemorrhages or bursts, but 
    it can physically alter the brain's structure over time, elevate your blood pressure 
    to dangerous levels, and indirectly contribute to nosebleeds.
